Massage for Runners

How therapeutic massage may support comfort, recovery, mobility, and consistent training throughout a runner's season.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

Running is beautifully simple: one foot in front of the other, repeated again and again. But that repetition also means the body absorbs thousands of loading cycles during a single training session.

Calves work to control and propel the body. The hips help stabilize each step. Hamstrings, quadriceps, glutes, feet, and trunk muscles coordinate to keep the runner moving efficiently. As mileage, speed, hills, or training frequency increase, those tissues may begin to feel tired, stiff, or sore.

Massage cannot replace appropriate training, sleep, nutrition, strength work, or medical care. It can, however, be one useful part of a broader recovery plan.

The quick answer

Massage may help runners feel less sore, more relaxed, and temporarily more comfortable moving.

Research on sports massage does not show that massage reliably improves running performance itself. The more consistent findings involve perceived recovery, soreness, and short-term flexibility.

What running asks of the body

Running is a repetitive loading activity. Each stride requires muscles and connective tissues to absorb force, stabilize joints, store and release energy, and produce forward movement.

That does not mean running is inherently damaging. The body is designed to adapt to load. Problems are more likely to develop when the amount or intensity of training exceeds what the body is currently prepared to tolerate.

Calves

The gastrocnemius and soleus help control the ankle and produce force during push-off. Hills, speed work, and increased mileage may leave the calves feeling especially fatigued.

Quadriceps

The quadriceps help control the knee and absorb load, particularly during downhill running and deceleration.

Hamstrings

The hamstrings contribute to hip extension, knee control, and the movement of the leg through the running cycle.

Glutes and hips

The gluteal and surrounding hip muscles help stabilize the pelvis and control the leg while the body is supported on one foot.

Feet and lower legs

The muscles of the feet and lower legs continually adjust to surface, speed, footwear, and impact.

Trunk

Running also requires coordinated trunk and pelvic control. Fatigue is not limited to the legs.

What massage may help with

The value of massage for a runner does not depend on forcing muscles to become "loose" or correcting every perceived imbalance.

A more realistic goal is to help the runner feel more comfortable, reduce unnecessary muscle guarding, and create an opportunity for recovery.

Post-exercise soreness

Research suggests massage may reduce the perception of delayed-onset muscle soreness after strenuous exercise.

Muscle tension

Comfortable hands-on work may help areas that feel tight or overworked settle after repeated training.

Short-term mobility

Some runners notice a temporary improvement in how easily they move after massage, particularly when stiffness has been limiting comfortable motion.

Relaxation

Training is not only physically demanding. Massage may provide time to slow down, rest, and shift out of a constantly "on" training mindset.

Body awareness

A session can give runners time to notice areas that feel unusually sensitive, tired, or restricted before returning to training.

Recovery routine

Massage can provide a structured recovery appointment in a schedule that might otherwise contain training but very little deliberate downtime.

Massage does not need to "flush out" your muscles

Runners sometimes hear that massage is necessary to remove lactic acid, flush toxins, or mechanically break apart tissue after exercise.

Those explanations oversimplify what is happening in the body.

The useful effects of massage are better understood in terms of comfort, sensation, relaxation, temporary changes in movement, and the experience of recovery—not physically squeezing waste products out of muscle.

Massage before a run or race

Timing matters.

A massage immediately before a race should generally feel very different from a long therapeutic session during a recovery week.

Shortly before competition

If massage is used shortly before running, the goal is usually to help the athlete feel prepared—not deeply worked.

  • Keep pressure comfortable.
  • Avoid creating soreness.
  • Favor shorter, stimulating work.
  • Pair it with the runner's normal warm-up.
  • Avoid introducing unfamiliar techniques on race day.
Race-week rule:

The closer you are to an important event, the less interested you should be in discovering how much pressure your legs can tolerate.

Massage after a hard run or race

After a long race, hard workout, or unfamiliar distance, the body may be tired, dehydrated, sore, and sensitive. That is not necessarily the best moment for aggressive bodywork.

Early recovery may simply include:

  • walking comfortably after finishing,
  • replacing fluids and food,
  • changing into dry clothing,
  • sleeping,
  • gentle movement as tolerated, and
  • allowing the initial fatigue to settle.

Massage later in the recovery period can then be adjusted to how the runner actually feels rather than automatically using deep pressure.

Common areas runners ask about

Calf tightness

Calf fatigue is common after hills, speed sessions, changes in footwear, or increases in training load. Massage may be comfortable for general muscular tightness, but sudden swelling or unexplained severe calf pain should not simply be massaged.

Hamstring tightness

Hamstrings may feel stiff after demanding workouts. Persistent focal pain, weakness, bruising, or pain following a sudden pull deserves further evaluation.

Hip tightness

The glutes, lateral hips, and surrounding tissues work continuously during running. Massage may help improve comfort, but strengthening and training decisions may matter more when symptoms repeatedly return.

IT band complaints

The iliotibial band is strong connective tissue. It does not need to be aggressively "rolled out." Work around the lateral hip and thigh should remain tolerable rather than becoming a contest in pain.

Foot fatigue

Foot and lower-leg massage can feel restorative after long periods of running, but persistent heel or arch pain may require more than massage alone.

Knee discomfort

Massage does not directly repair the structures of the knee. Work on surrounding muscles may sometimes improve comfort, but ongoing knee pain deserves an appropriate assessment.

Can massage prevent running injuries?

It is tempting to think of massage as preventive maintenance: keep the muscles loose and injuries will not happen.

The reality is more complicated.

Running injuries can be influenced by training load, tissue capacity, previous injury, strength, recovery, sleep, biomechanics, terrain, footwear, and many other factors. Massage cannot control all of those variables.

Massage may support a runner's recovery routine, but it should not be marketed as an injury-prevention guarantee.

Consistent training, appropriate progression, strength work, adequate recovery, and responding appropriately to persistent symptoms remain important.

Does a runner need deep tissue massage?

No.

Some runners enjoy firm therapeutic work. Others recover better with moderate pressure. The appropriate session depends on the runner, timing, goals, sensitivity, recent training, and how the tissues respond.

More pressure does not automatically mean more effective treatment.

A useful session should be tolerable.

If you are bracing, holding your breath, tightening the entire leg, or waiting for the massage to end, reducing the pressure may actually allow more productive work.

How often should runners get massage?

There is no medically required massage schedule for runners.

Frequency depends on goals, training volume, budget, preference, and how useful the runner finds massage.

Recreational runners

Some runners schedule massage monthly or simply when training volume increases or a particularly demanding event is approaching.

Higher-volume training

Runners in heavier training blocks may prefer sessions every two to four weeks, depending on recovery needs and individual response.

Race preparation

Massage can be scheduled strategically around long runs, taper periods, or recovery days rather than automatically following a fixed calendar.

After an event

The timing and intensity of post-race massage should reflect how the athlete feels rather than assuming every tired muscle needs deep treatment.

When massage should wait

Running soreness and an injury are not the same thing. Massage should not be used to push through symptoms that need medical evaluation.

Pause massage and seek appropriate medical guidance for concerns such as:

  • suspected fracture,
  • significant swelling after an injury,
  • inability to bear weight normally,
  • new numbness or significant weakness,
  • open wounds or active skin infection,
  • fever or systemic illness,
  • severe or rapidly worsening pain,
  • unexplained one-sided calf swelling, warmth, redness, or significant tenderness.
Important:

One-sided calf pain and swelling can sometimes indicate a blood clot. That requires prompt medical evaluation, not massage.

Build recovery around more than massage

Massage works best as one tool inside a broader recovery strategy.

Sleep

Consistent sleep supports recovery in ways no recovery gadget or treatment can replace.

Food

Training creates energy demands. Adequate nutrition supports adaptation and recovery.

Hydration

Replace fluids according to your environment, training duration, sweat rate, and individual needs.

Strength

Muscles and connective tissues benefit from adequate capacity, not simply from being stretched or massaged.

Easy days

Every training day does not need to be difficult. Appropriate variation helps manage accumulated load.

Communication

Tell your massage therapist about recent races, mileage changes, current soreness, injuries, and what you want from the session.

What should you tell your massage therapist?

The more context your therapist has, the easier it is to adapt the session appropriately.

  • What distance are you currently training for?
  • When was your last hard workout or race?
  • When is your next important run?
  • Have you recently changed mileage or intensity?
  • Are any areas painful rather than simply sore?
  • Have you had previous running injuries?
  • What pressure usually feels productive to you?
  • Is your goal relaxation, general recovery, or focused work on a particular area?

The takeaway

Running asks the body to tolerate repeated load, and recovery is part of the training process—not something separate from it.

Massage may help reduce perceived soreness, improve comfort, temporarily support flexibility, and create intentional recovery time.

It does not replace smart training, strength, sleep, nutrition, or appropriate medical care.

The best massage for a runner is not necessarily the deepest session. It is the session that matches the athlete's goals, training schedule, current condition, and individual response.
