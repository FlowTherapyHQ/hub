Should I Go to the ER? Understanding Stress, Pain, and When to Seek Care By Brandy Hennigan, LMT There are moments when seeking emergency medical care is absolutely the right decision. Chest pain, difficulty breathing, sudden weakness, severe injuries, or signs of stroke should never be ignored. But many people find themselves wondering about symptoms that feel alarming but aren't necessarily emergencies. Muscle tension, headaches, back pain, neck pain, dizziness, racing thoughts, chest tightness, or overwhelming anxiety can all feel frightening—and chronic stress can make those symptoms even more intense. Learning when to seek emergency care, when to contact your healthcare provider, and how to manage everyday stress can help you make informed decisions while taking better care of your body. When You Should Go to the Emergency Room Seek immediate medical attention or call 911 if you experience symptoms such as: Chest pain, pressure, or discomfort that could indicate a heart attack Difficulty breathing or severe shortness of breath Sudden numbness or weakness, especially on one side of the body Difficulty speaking or understanding speech Sudden confusion Loss of consciousness Severe bleeding Major injuries or broken bones Seizures Severe allergic reactions Thoughts of harming yourself or others, or a mental health crisis where you feel you may be in immediate danger When in doubt, it is always safer to seek emergency medical evaluation. Stress Can Feel Like an Emergency Stress and anxiety don't only affect the mind—they affect the entire body. Stress can contribute to: Muscle tension Neck and shoulder pain Tension headaches Jaw pain or clenching Trouble sleeping Digestive discomfort Rapid heartbeat Chest tightness Fatigue Difficulty concentrating Sometimes these symptoms are frightening enough that people visit an emergency department to make sure nothing more serious is happening. According to the CDC, millions of emergency department visits each year involve mental and behavioral health conditions, including anxiety and stress-related disorders. While emergency care is essential for true medical emergencies, many people also benefit from ongoing stress management strategies and appropriate follow-up care after serious conditions have been ruled out. Massage Therapy Is Preventive Healthcare Massage therapy is not a substitute for emergency medical care, and it should never delay treatment for serious symptoms. However, research suggests that therapeutic massage can help many people: Reduce perceived stress Decrease anxiety Improve sleep quality Reduce muscle tension Ease headaches Improve relaxation Support overall well-being Think of massage the same way you think about exercise, healthy eating, and adequate sleep. It's one part of a proactive approach to caring for your health before stress, tension, and discomfort become overwhelming. A Different Way to Think About Wellness Many of us wait until our bodies demand attention. We push through headaches. Ignore stiff shoulders. Work through back pain. Tell ourselves we'll slow down "next week." Eventually, the body gets louder. While massage therapy can't prevent every illness or emergency, regularly caring for your body may help reduce the physical effects of chronic stress and encourage you to address concerns before they become bigger problems. Wellness isn't about perfection—it's about consistency. When Massage May Be Helpful Massage therapy may be appropriate if you're experiencing: ✔ Stress-related muscle tension ✔ Desk or computer posture discomfort ✔ Headaches related to muscle tension ✔ Recovery from exercise ✔ Difficulty relaxing ✔ General aches and stiffness ✔ Ongoing wellness maintenance If your symptoms are severe, worsening, or accompanied by warning signs such as chest pain, difficulty breathing, fever, sudden weakness, or significant injury, seek medical evaluation first. Final Thoughts Your body is remarkably good at communicating when it needs attention. Sometimes that means calling 911. Sometimes it means scheduling an appointment with your healthcare provider. Sometimes it means taking a walk, improving your sleep, practicing deep breathing, or scheduling a therapeutic massage. Listening to your body before small problems become larger ones is one of the greatest investments you can make in your long-term health. 💙 Did You Know?
